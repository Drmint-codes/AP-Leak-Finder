The short version

The Association of Certified Fraud Examiners’ Occupational Fraud 2026 covers 2,402 cases from 143 countries and territories, with total losses of more than $3.4 billion. Billing schemes appeared in 21% of them, and a typical case lasted 12 months before detection.[1, p. 4]

This is a fraud study, and most payment mistakes aren’t fraud. It’s still useful here, because it measures how long problems last and which ordinary controls went with smaller losses.

What the study covers

The report analyzes real cases investigated between January 2024 and September 2025.[1, p. 6] Across them, the median loss was $104,000 and the average was $1,457,000.[1, p. 4] That gap shows the shape of the problem: most cases are moderate, and a few are very large.

The certified fraud examiners surveyed also estimate that organizations lose 5% of revenue to fraud each year.[1, p. 4] That’s an estimate from practitioners, not a measured figure.

Schemes that run through payables

ACFE breaks asset misappropriation into nine schemes. Two of them run directly through accounts payable: billing schemes, and check and payment tampering.[1, p. 15]

Check and payment tampering had the highest median loss of the nine, and billing schemes were the second most common. Both lasted longer than the 12-month median for all cases.[1, p. 15][1, p. 18]

More than half of tips came from employees, and almost one-third came from vendors and customers.[1, p. 4] ACFE also found that fraud can be uncovered up to four times faster with active detection methods than with passive ones.[1, p. 24]

Which controls went with lower losses

ACFE compares organizations with and without each of 18 anti-fraud controls. All 18 were associated with lower median losses and faster detection, with losses 37% lower on average.[1, p. 39] Three of the strongest are close to ordinary bookkeeping practice.

The weakness behind cases points the same way. A lack of internal controls was the primary weakness in one-third of cases, and overriding existing controls in another 19%.[1, p. 49] Where a lack of controls was the primary factor, median losses were 43% higher than overall.[1, p. 39] After the fraud, 80% of victim organizations changed their controls.[1, p. 5]

Small businesses carry more of the risk

Organizations with fewer than 100 employees had the highest median loss of any size group, at $126,000.[1, p. 5] ACFE notes that the effect of a loss is typically more significant at smaller organizations, since it’s likely a larger share of revenue.[1, p. 32]

Billing schemes appeared in 28% of their cases, compared with 18% at organizations with 100 or more employees. Check and payment tampering appeared in 18%, compared with 8%.[1, p. 34] They’re also much less likely to have controls in place: 85% of larger organizations have a whistleblowing mechanism, against only one-quarter of small businesses.[1, p. 44]

What this data can’t tell you

  • It only covers known cases. These are frauds that were found and investigated, so undetected fraud isn’t in the sample.
  • It shows association, not cause. Organizations that run surprise audits may differ in other ways too, so a control’s difference isn’t proof of what it would do for you.
  • It’s about fraud. The figures don’t describe the rate of honest mistakes like a bill keyed twice.

Where Reclaim fits

Reclaim isn’t a fraud tool, and it doesn’t decide whether anything is fraud. It reads an export of your payments and points to ones that don’t line up, like repeated payments or changed vendor bank details, so someone can look sooner. Whether a result is a mistake or something else is for the people who know the vendor to decide.
