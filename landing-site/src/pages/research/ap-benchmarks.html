The short version

Two published benchmarks describe how often accounts payable gets a payment or invoice wrong. APQC’s median is 1.5% of disbursements.[1] Ardent Partners’ average invoice exception rate is 18.4%.[2]

Neither is a loss figure, and they don’t measure the same thing. One counts payments that already went out wrong. The other counts invoices that couldn’t be processed cleanly. Reading them correctly matters more than the numbers themselves.

What APQC measures

APQC’s measure is the percentage of the total annual number of disbursements that are duplicate or erroneous payments. It defines a disbursement as a single remittance to a supplier.[1] So this is a count of payments, not a share of dollars.

The public page shows a 1.5% median across a total sample of 2,386 companies. The 25th and 75th percentile values aren’t shown publicly.[1]

APQC scopes the measure to steps including verifying the pay file against the vendor master, auditing invoices and key data, approving payments, and researching and resolving payable exceptions.[1]

What 1.5% looks like

This is our own arithmetic, not APQC’s: at the median rate, an organization issuing 4,000 payments a year would expect about 60 to be duplicate or erroneous. A duplicate on a $400 bill and a duplicate on a $40,000 invoice each count as one, so the rate alone tells you nothing about the money.

What Ardent Partners measures

Ardent’s 18.4% is the average share of invoices that need exception handling.[2] An exception is an invoice that stops, not necessarily one that was paid wrong. Ardent calls exceptions typically the biggest single reason its other benchmarks aren’t better.[2]

Ardent also reports that 39% of AP leaders believe their potential is held back by the volume of manual, low-value work.[2]

What the best teams do differently

Ardent defines Best-in-Class as the 20% of enterprises with the lowest average invoice processing costs and the shortest cycle times, and notes that these groups use technology to streamline AP.[2]

Because Best-in-Class is defined by cost and speed, those two gaps partly follow from how the group was chosen. The exception rate gap is the more telling one for this topic.

Why the two numbers don’t combine

It’s tempting to read these as one story, but they measure different moments. APQC looks at payments after they’ve gone out. Ardent looks at invoices before they’re paid. An organization could do well on one and poorly on the other.

Our reading, not a finding from either source, is that they’re connected through manual work: invoices that fall out of the normal flow get handled by hand, and handling by hand creates more chances for a mistake.

What this data can’t tell you

  • It isn’t a loss figure. APQC’s number counts payments, not dollars.[1]
  • It doesn’t describe your company. Both are figures taken across a group, and your own rate could be far higher or lower.
  • The detail is limited. APQC’s full benchmark data is for its members.[1] The Ardent article we cite doesn’t state its sample size or methods; it attributes its findings to Ardent’s State of ePayables 2025 report.[2]

What to do with it

  • Count first, then size. Find out how many payments are in question and which vendors they sit with before you ask what it cost.
  • Group exceptions by reason. A missing purchase order and a price mismatch are different problems with different fixes.
  • Check your vendor list. The same vendor set up twice is an easy way for one invoice to get paid twice.
  • Look at your own records. A benchmark tells you what’s worth checking, not what happened to you.
