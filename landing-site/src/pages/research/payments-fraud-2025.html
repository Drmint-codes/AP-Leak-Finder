The short version

The Association for Financial Professionals found that 76% of US organizations experienced attempted or actual payments fraud in 2025.[1] The FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center counted 1,008,597 complaints and $20.877 billion in reported losses, up 26% on 2024.[2, p. 6]

These come from opposite ends and shouldn’t be added together. AFP asks organizations whether fraud was attempted, whether or not money moved. IC3 counts what people chose to report losing, across every kind of internet crime.

The AFP report was underwritten by Truist.[1] We cite AFP’s published summary. IC3 says it now averages almost 3,000 complaints a day.[2, p. 3]

Which payment methods get targeted

Checks are still the most targeted method.[1]

Checks are also hard to drop. Of organizations that use them, 72% plan to keep doing so for the foreseeable future, and 68% of those cite vendor requirements.[1] So the most attacked method is partly out of the payer’s hands.

Business email compromise

AFP found 74% of organizations were affected by business email compromise in 2025, a significant increase from 2023 and 2024.[1] At IC3, it was the second-largest loss category of the year.[2, p. 9]

The FBI defines it as a scam targeting businesses or individuals working with suppliers or regularly making wire transfers, carried out by compromising email and other channels through social engineering or computer intrusion, to make an unauthorized transfer of funds.[2, p. 59]

About $30 million of 2025’s BEC losses were reported as involving AI.[2, p. 42] For scale, IC3’s largest category was investment fraud at $8.65 billion, and tech support scams came after BEC at $2.13 billion.[2, p. 9] The $20.877 billion total covers far more than fraud aimed at business payments.

Who loses money, and who notices

Attempts don’t always become losses, and size matters. 48% of organizations with revenue under $1 billion reported financial losses from fraud in 2025, compared with 66% of those above it.[1]

Treasury is the department most likely to discover both attempted fraud, at 83%, and actual fraud, at 55%.[1]

How far defenses have come

Only 17% of organizations use AI to combat payments fraud. Of those that do, 49% report more efficient fraud reporting, 45% better detection of deepfakes, and 43% better real-time identification.[1] These are the users’ own reports of benefit, not measured outcomes.

What this data can’t tell you

  • Neither is a rate of loss. AFP counts organizations that saw fraud at all, including attempts that failed.
  • IC3 only sees what gets reported. It’s a count of complaints, not of all fraud.
  • Neither describes your company. AFP surveyed treasury practitioners, and IC3 counts whoever filed.

What to do with it

  • Keep a list of vendor changes. Every change to bank details is a record with a date. A real change and an attacker’s change can look the same at first, so confirm through a contact you already trust.
  • Match checks to bills. Each check should tie to one bill, and each bill to one check.
  • Watch for repeated payments. In a ledger, a payment run that went out twice looks the same whatever caused it.
  • Make it quick to answer questions about a payment. It helps when the people who find problems can pull the records fast.

Where Reclaim fits

Reclaim isn’t a fraud tool. One of its eight checks flags when a vendor’s deposit account changes between payments, and others flag repeated payments, but it reads the export you give it and can’t tell you who was behind a change. It shows you the records, and you confirm with the vendor.
