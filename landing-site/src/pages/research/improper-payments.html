The short version

The US Government Accountability Office reports that 15 federal agencies estimated about $186 billion in improper payments across 64 programs in fiscal 2025. About $153 billion of that, roughly 82%, was overpayments.[1, p. 1][1, p. 2]

Federal programs aren’t companies. This report is useful because it’s one of the few places where payments that went wrong are estimated, published and reviewed every year.

What counts as improper

GAO describes improper payments as those that should not have been made or were made in incorrect amounts.[1, p. 1] That’s broader than fraud, and it’s not the same as money lost.

The estimates also treat as improper any payment whose propriety can’t be determined because documentation is lacking or insufficient. GAO calls these unknown payments.[1, p. 3] Besides overpayments, the total includes underpayments, unknown payments and technically improper payments.[1, p. 3]

Where the money concentrates

About 73%, or roughly $136 billion, sits in five program areas: Medicare ($57 billion across three programs), Medicaid ($37 billion), the Earned Income Tax Credit ($21 billion), SNAP ($10 billion) and the Shuttered Venue Operators Grant ($10 billion).[1, p. 2]

A high rate and a large amount are different things. 19 programs reported rates of at least 10%, and six exceeded 25%.[1, p. 5] Medicaid has the largest amount at a 6.1% rate, while the Shuttered Venue program has a 68.9% rate on a much smaller amount.

Why the total went up

The total rose about $24 billion from fiscal 2024. GAO attributes this largely to programs that reported estimates in 2025 but not the year before, and also notes an increase in Medicaid.[1, p. 1]

So part of the rise is more being measured, not necessarily more going wrong. GAO also notes that every estimate has a margin of error, which could explain part of the changes.[1, p. 7][1, p. 8]

One decline had a stated cause: Medicare Fee-for-Service fell by $2.9 billion, which HHS attributed to stronger internal controls around prior authorizations, which check rules before services are rendered and claims are paid.[1, p. 7]

What the estimate leaves out

GAO is explicit that $186 billion isn’t the full extent of improper payments.[1, p. 1] For example, it excludes Temporary Assistance for Needy Families, which spent about $16.5 billion in fiscal 2025. HHS says it doesn’t calculate an amount for that program because of statutory limitations.[1, p. 3]

Inspectors general have also reported that estimates are unreliable for some programs.[1, p. 2] Since fiscal 2003, the cumulative estimate is about $3 trillion, and GAO says the actual amount may be significantly higher.[1, p. 1]

How agencies are doing

In fiscal 2024, the latest year available, 12 of the 24 agencies covered by the Chief Financial Officers Act fully complied with the payment integrity criteria, according to their inspectors general. That’s down from 13 the year before.[1, p. 11]

Inspectors general made 61 recommendations to 13 agencies that year, 20 of them repeated from earlier years.[1, p. 14] Root causes were the largest category of open recommendations, at 24.[1, p. 15] Inspectors general also flagged inadequate risk assessments at five agencies and unreliable estimates at seven.[2]

GAO recommended 10 matters for Congress in March 2022. One became law in February 2026, making permanent a pilot that shares the Social Security Death Master File with Treasury’s Do Not Pay system. The other nine remain open.[1, p. 4]

What this data can’t tell you

  • It isn’t about businesses. A Medicaid eligibility error has little in common with a duplicate bill from a supplier.
  • Improper doesn’t mean fraud or lost. The total includes underpayments and payments that simply couldn’t be verified.
  • These are estimates. They have margins of error, and methods can change from year to year.

What a business can take from it

These are our own takeaways, not GAO’s recommendations for companies.

  • Overpayments can dominate. Most of the federal total was money that went out when it shouldn’t have, or in too large an amount.
  • A few sources can hold most of the money. It’s worth checking whether a handful of your vendors account for most of your problems.
  • Rate and amount answer different questions. One points to process quality, the other to how much is at stake.
  • Unmeasured doesn’t mean fine. A vendor nobody has reconciled isn’t a vendor without problems.
  • Documentation matters. A payment you can’t verify is a question you can’t answer.
